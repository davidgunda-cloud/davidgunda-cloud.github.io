Welcome · About the study

Selection, not just consumption

Ungulates don't eat plants in proportion to how common they are. They select. This project asks which plants browsers and grazers choose through the year, and why some species are favoured over others.

The work brings together fieldwork in an African savanna and a European oak forest, to understand how wild herbivores use the vegetation around them.

What we want to know

Research questions

1

Which plants, and which parts?

Which plant species and plant parts do wild ungulates use most, and how does this change through the year?

2

Is the most common plant the most eaten?

Do ungulates mainly eat the most abundant plants, or do they seek out particular species however common they are?

3

What makes a plant attractive?

Do plant form, seasonal state and food quality help explain why some species are favoured over others?

The field app

DaviGherbivores

Built for this project as an alternative to generic survey forms, DaviGherbivores helps the field team record observations and photos at remote sites and keeps everyone's records together. It can be used at study sites anywhere in the world.

  • Works offlineRecords saved on the phone, synced later
  • GPS at every plotLocation straight from the phone
  • Photos with recordsTaken in the app, kept with each record
  • Team syncEveryone's records in one place
  • Private by designOnly the project team can sign in
  • Any study siteSites and species can be added freely

The researcher

Daud Mathew Gunda

Wildlife ecologist · PhD researcher, Hungarian University of Agriculture and Life Sciences (MATE) · also known as David

Daud Gunda is a wildlife ecologist with more than eight years of experience in wildlife conservation. His work spans wildlife ecology, human–wildlife conflict, community conservation, tourism management, anti-poaching, wildlife research and database management.

He holds an MSc in Wildlife Management Engineering from the Hungarian University of Agriculture and Life Sciences (MATE). Before that, he trained for six consecutive years at the College of African Wildlife Management, Mweka, in Moshi, Kilimanjaro, Tanzania, progressing from a Certificate and an Ordinary Diploma to a Bachelor's degree in Wildlife Management, all taught in English.

Since completing his BSc, he has published five articles in peer-reviewed journals, several with colleagues, on topics from large mammal distribution and human–wildlife conflict to reptiles and ground-dwelling arthropods. His PhD research examines how wild ungulates select their food according to plant species composition, phenology and nutritional quality, in Tanzania and Hungary.
